## Executive Summary

The supplied October 2026 papers suggest that AI in materials science is advancing from isolated property predictors toward integrated discovery workflows, physically structured generative models, and more carefully validated atomistic simulation. Agentic systems target grain-boundary research, electromagnetic inverse design, and scientific computation, while crystal generators increasingly represent disorder, symmetry, magnetic moments, and structural constraints explicitly. Machine-learned interatomic potentials support defect and transformation studies, but several papers emphasize that low reference-data errors do not guarantee stable dynamics or accurate thermodynamics. Data quality is an equally important theme: literature-mining pipelines, dynamical-stability datasets, and chronology-aware synthesizability models address different bottlenecks between computational candidates and useful materials. - Resource-aware discovery: active learning, adaptive Bayesian optimization, uncertainty-aware solvent screening, and chronology-aware synthesizability prediction seek to allocate expensive calculations and experiments more effectively.

## Field Progress

- Generative modeling is broadening the design variables it can represent, moving beyond ordered atomic coordinates toward disorder, magnetic moments, structural frameworks, and microstructural geometry.
- Discovery workflows increasingly combine proposals with external checks. Reaction-path verification, full-wave electromagnetic simulation, and finite-element-guided scaffold refinement illustrate different ways to connect generation to physical evaluation.
- Atomistic AI is becoming more application-specific: defect-focused fine-tuning, reactive polymer modeling, grain-boundary segregation studies, and phase-stability corrections address weaknesses that broad pretraining alone may leave unresolved.
- Evaluation is expanding from pointwise prediction to scientific usefulness, including stable molecular dynamics, preservation of elastic and vibrational behavior, distinct-candidate yield, and reproducible computational workflows.
- Materials data engineering is receiving greater methodological attention, with efforts to improve dynamical-stability coverage, document extraction provenance, reject physically inconsistent values, and evaluate property models across held-out materials.
- Inverse design is extending across scales, from crystal composition and reaction networks to alloy-processing recipes and printable scaffold microstructures. The supplied abstracts do not establish widespread experimental or industrial deployment.

## Open Questions

- Which combinations of physical checks, expert oversight, and recovery mechanisms make agentic workflows reliably correct rather than merely executable?
- How should atomistic foundation models be benchmarked so that low energy and force errors predict stable trajectories, credible reaction pathways, and accurate phase behavior?
- Can disorder-aware, magnetic, and symmetry-constrained generators improve experimentally realizable candidate yield under fixed validation budgets, rather than only increasing computational screening pass rates?
- How can literature-derived datasets quantify residual error after verification, preserve source-level provenance, and avoid correlated mistakes across apparently independent publications?
- How much of chronology-based synthesizability prediction reflects chemistry, and how much reflects historical funding, instrumentation, reporting practices, or researcher preferences?
- Can generated microstructures and learned inverse-processing models generalize across imaging conditions, alloy families, manufacturing routes, and loading regimes with calibrated uncertainty?

## Outlook

The most consequential next step is to connect these methodological advances to independently validated discovery outcomes. Promising directions include combining structured generators with dynamical-stability and synthesizability screening, coupling specialized atomistic models to uncertainty-driven reference calculations, and giving agents explicit physical constraints and auditable workflow checkpoints. Progress should be measured by validated candidate yield, experimental transfer, stable simulation, and total resource cost—not just prediction accuracy or generation volume. Across the supplied papers, the central message is that broader AI capability and stronger physical accountability need to advance together.
